1. What is Nitinol?

Nitinol is a metal alloy of roughly equal parts nickel (Ni) and titanium (Ti). It is famous for shape memory and superelasticity.

Normal wire: bend it and it stays bent. Nitinol wire: bend it while cool, heat it up, and it forcefully snaps back to its original, manufactured shape.

2. How Nitinol works

Explanation level:

Step 1: the same atoms, two patterns

Hot: atoms sit in a neat, cube-like pattern called austenite. Cold: the pattern is tilted and squashed, called martensite. Drag the crystal to rotate it, then slide between hot and cold.

AusteniteCubic (B2)

Blue = Ni, orange = Ti. Distortion is exaggerated to be visible.

Engineering detail: austenite is B2 (CsCl-type) cubic, Ni at the corners and Ti at the body centre. Martensite is B19′, a monoclinic distortion. Lower symmetry means several equivalent orientations ("variants", up to 12 lattice-correspondence variants) that can stack as twins. Try "Bend it": the variants line up in one direction.

Step 2: temperature decides the phase

Heat favours austenite, cold favours martensite. Slide the temperature, or switch on an electric current to heat the wire. Watch the chart: the way up is not the same as the way down. That gap is called hysteresis.

Temperatures are illustrative. The current heats fast and the wire cools slowly (time-lapse ×3). In spring mode the weight stretches the cold, soft spring. Heating makes it contract and lift the weight.

Engineering detail: four temperatures describe the loop: Mf < Ms < As < Af (martensite finish/start on cooling, austenite start/finish on heating). Stress shifts them upward by roughly 6–8 MPa per °C, which is why a loaded wire above Af can still transform (superelasticity, section 3). Actuator wires are tuned with Ms above room temperature so they reset after cooling.

Step 3: the shape-memory cycle

Press play. Watch the zig-zag layers in the crystal and the wire at the bottom.

Engineering detail: cooling forms self-accommodating twinned martensite, so there is no shape change. Bending moves the twin boundaries (detwinning) rather than creating dislocations, and can absorb about 6–8% strain in NiTi. Heating returns every variant to the single parent austenite orientation, so the original shape is recovered. Designers usually keep working strain to about 2–4% for long fatigue life.

Step 4: why can it do this again and again?

Think of a folding chair: it opens and closes without anything breaking. Nitinol's atoms shift together by less than one atom spacing, so they keep the same neighbours. Compare it with an ordinary metal, where layers slip past each other.

Engineering detail: the transformation is diffusionless and displacive (cooperative shear). Ordinary metals yield by dislocation slip, which leaves a permanent offset. Because the NiTi transformation is crystallographically reversible, damage does not accumulate in the same way, though cyclic fatigue still limits life.

3. Two superpowers

🔥 Shape memory effect

  • 1. Make the part in its desired shape (hot, austenite).
  • 2. Cool it to martensite and deform it. It stays deformed.
  • 3. Heat above the transition. It returns to the original shape, with real force.

The trigger is temperature.

🌀 Superelasticity

Above Af, the load itself turns austenite into martensite, giving huge recoverable strain. Remove the load and it springs back, no heating needed. Press play and compare with steel.

4. Where is it used?

Tap a card. Because its transition can be tuned to about 37 °C, Nitinol is popular in medicine.

A collapsed stent is pushed through a narrow vessel. In the warmth of the bloodstream it expands to a tube and props the artery open.

Engineering note: many real stents rely mainly on superelasticity, springing open when released from the delivery catheter.

Archwires give a constant, gentle pressure as the warmth of the mouth tries to pull the wire back to its straight shape.

Nitinol wire acts like artificial muscle: it contracts when heated by electric current and relaxes when it cools. Cooling time limits cycle speed.

Using superelasticity, frames can be bent or sat on and then spring back without breaking.

5. The engineer's view

Nitinol is impressive, but it is not a drop-in replacement for steel.

  • Recovers large strains (far beyond normal metals)
  • Produces force on heating (actuators)
  • Transition temperature can be tuned
  • Works in the body when processed and surface treated
  • One part can replace several (springs, hinges, latches)
  • More expensive than common alloys
  • Harder to machine, weld and heat treat
  • Transition temperature is very sensitive to composition
  • Cooling limits actuator speed
  • Fatigue life must be checked

Design checklist

Compare service temperature with As and Af. Below As: shape memory. Above Af: superelastic.
Repeated loading causes fatigue. Keep strains moderate for long life.
Use it where its behaviour removes parts or enables a function nothing else can.

7. Summary

Key takeaways

  • Nitinol ≈ 50% Ni + 50% Ti.
  • Cold = martensite (soft, twinned). Hot = austenite (cubic, remembered shape).
  • Transformation temperatures: Mf < Ms < As < Af, with hysteresis between heating and cooling.
  • Shape memory: heat triggers recovery. Superelasticity: unloading triggers recovery (above Af).
  • Atoms shift cooperatively without breaking bonds, so it repeats.
  • Great for stents, braces, actuators, frames; costly and harder to process.
